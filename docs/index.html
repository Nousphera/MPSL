Fine-Tuning Multimodal Transformers on Edge: A Parallel Split Learning Approach

Multimodal fine-tuning with lightweight edge clients.

Timo Fudala · Vasileios Tsouvalas · Nirvana Meratnia

The challenge

Multimodal fine-tuning can exceed client compute budgets even when only a small part of the model is trainable.

The contribution

The idea

At a glance

Multimodal transformers are expensive to fine-tune on edge devices, even when only a small fraction of their parameters changes. MPSL keeps modality-specific tokenizers on clients and moves a shared encoder to the server, coordinating learning through aggregated local losses while supporting different combinations of images, audio and text.

  • Offloading a frozen or trainable encoder can save client computation that parameter-efficient federated tuning still requires locally.
  • Batch size and fusion strategy matter: neither distributed retrieval nor multimodal fusion has one universally best setting.

  • Parallel split learning
  • Edge AI
Read the abstract

Multimodal transformers integrate diverse data types like images, audio, and text, advancing tasks such as audio-visual understanding and image-text retrieval; yet their high parameterization limits deployment on resource-constrained edge devices. Split Learning (SL), which partitions models at a designated cut-layer to offload compute-intensive operations to the server, offers a promising approach for distributed training of multimodal transformers, though its application remains underexplored. We present MPSL, a parallel SL approach for computational efficient fine-tuning of multimodal transformers in a distributed manner, while eliminating label sharing, client synchronization, and per-client sub-model management. MPSL employs lightweight client-side tokenizers and a unified modality-agnostic encoder, allowing flexible adaptation to task-specific needs. Our evaluation across 7 multimodal datasets demonstrates that MPSL matches or outperforms Federated Learning, reduces client-side computations by 250×, and achieves superior scalability in communication cost with model growth. Through extensive analysis, we highlight task suitability, trade-offs, and scenarios where MPSL excels, inspiring further exploration.

The approach

How it works

  1. 01

    Tokenize each modality locally

    Convert images, audio or text into token representations on the client. Send these activations to the server instead of running the full transformer locally.

  2. 02

    Share one multimodal encoder

    Process client activations through a common encoder. Fuse modalities before or after encoding according to the downstream task, then return predictions to clients.

  3. 03

    Train with aggregated local losses

    Clients compute losses using their local labels. The server aggregates the losses, performs a shared backward pass and returns cut-layer gradients to update the client tokenizers.

Experiments & evidence

What the results show

Separate client compute savings from task accuracy, and distinguish raw retrieval recall from scores normalized to centralized training.

Data
COCO-QA, T4SA, Kinetics-Sounds, UCF101, MELD, MS-COCO and Flickr30K; image/text, image/audio and audio/text pairs.
Models
Meta-Transformer ViT-B/16; encoder-size analysis spans ViT-Tiny to ViT-Huge. Classification typically tunes the last six blocks; retrieval tunes the complete encoder.
Compared with
Centralized fine-tuning, FedAvg and FedCLIP; 25 or 100 clients with non-IID class partitions.
Evaluation model
Client tokenizers are aggregated after training and combined with the shared server model for comparison with federated baselines.

Scope & limitations

  • Accuracy is task-dependent: client compute savings do not imply matching FedAvg or centralized performance on every benchmark.
  • Retrieval needs sufficiently large batches; limited client data can make larger batch settings infeasible.
  • Missing modalities, client dropouts and heterogeneous edge hardware are explicitly left for future work.
  • Keeping raw labels local does not itself establish a formal privacy guarantee, and reported client GFLOPs do not establish end-to-end speed or energy gains.
