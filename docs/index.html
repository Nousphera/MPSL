Fine-Tuning Multimodal Transformers on Edge: A Parallel Split Learning Approach

Multimodal fine-tuning with lightweight edge clients.

Timo Fudala · Vasileios Tsouvalas · Nirvana Meratnia

PaperCodePoster

At a glance

Multimodal fine-tuning can overwhelm an edge device even when few parameters change. MPSL keeps lightweight tokenizers on clients and runs a shared transformer on the server, coordinating training through local losses and returned gradients.

What this adds

  • 01

    Multimodal splitting: support paired image, audio and text inputs with one shared encoder.

  • 02

    Shared optimization: aggregate client losses for one server backward pass while keeping raw labels local.

  • 03

    Task-dependent evidence: measure accuracy, retrieval, client compute and communication across seven datasets.

Abstract

Multimodal transformers integrate diverse data types like images, audio, and text, advancing tasks such as audio-visual understanding and image-text retrieval; yet their high parameterization limits deployment on resource-constrained edge devices. Split Learning (SL), which partitions models at a designated cut-layer to offload compute-intensive operations to the server, offers a promising approach for distributed training of multimodal transformers, though its application remains underexplored. We present MPSL, a parallel SL approach for computational efficient fine-tuning of multimodal transformers in a distributed manner, while eliminating label sharing, client synchronization, and per-client sub-model management. MPSL employs lightweight client-side tokenizers and a unified modality-agnostic encoder, allowing flexible adaptation to task-specific needs. Our evaluation across 7 multimodal datasets demonstrates that MPSL matches or outperforms Federated Learning, reduces client-side computations by 250×, and achieves superior scalability in communication cost with model growth. Through extensive analysis, we highlight task suitability, trade-offs, and scenarios where MPSL excels, inspiring further exploration.

How it works

MPSL

01

Tokenize locally

Image patches, audio spectrogram patches and text tokens provide compatible inputs to a modality-agnostic transformer. Clients run the tokenizers; the server runs the encoder and task head.

Section 3.1–3.2 and Figure 1 · public preprint, pp. 2–4.

02

Fuse at the right stage

Early fusion concatenates token sequences before encoding. Late fusion encodes modalities independently with shared weights and concatenates their representations afterward. The useful choice depends on the task and modality pair.

Section 3.2 and Table 5 · pp. 3 and 7.

03

Minimize the combined client loss

Predictions return to clients for loss calculation. The server weights each loss by its share of the global batch, backpropagates once, then returns cut-layer gradients to the local tokenizers.

ℒS=∑n∈𝒩|ℬn||ℬ|ℒCn

Equation (4), p. 4. ℬn is client n’s batch; ℬ is the combined batch. This avoids client-weight synchronization during training, not all coordination.

04

Construct the evaluation model

After training, the reported comparisons average the client tokenizers and join them with the shared server model. This post-training aggregation is distinct from the loss-based training loop.

Section 3.3 · p. 4.

Takeaways

  • Client compute falls sharply: classification uses 0.1 versus FedAvg’s 25.8 GFLOPs per input; this does not measure total system cost.
  • Accuracy remains task-dependent: MPSL improves MELD but trails FedAvg on COCO-QA and Kinetics-Sounds in the selected comparison.
  • Retrieval needs enough batch diversity: it improves over the evaluated distributed baselines but remains below centralized fine-tuning.
Study scope & sources
  • Seven paired-modality datasets; missing modalities and client dropouts are future work.
  • Client GFLOPs are not end-to-end latency, energy or server cost.
  • Raw recall and normalized recall belong to different experimental settings.
Data
COCO-QA, T4SA, Kinetics-Sounds, UCF101, MELD, MS-COCO and Flickr30K; image/text, image/audio and audio/text pairs.
Models
Meta-Transformer ViT-B/16; encoder-size analysis spans ViT-Tiny to ViT-Huge. Classification typically tunes the last six blocks; retrieval tunes the complete encoder.
Compared with
Centralized fine-tuning, FedAvg and FedCLIP; 25 or 100 clients with non-IID class partitions.
Evaluation model
Client tokenizers are aggregated after training and combined with the shared server model for comparison with federated baselines.

Scope & limitations

  • Accuracy is task-dependent: client compute savings do not imply matching FedAvg or centralized performance on every benchmark.
  • Retrieval needs sufficiently large batches; limited client data can make larger batch settings infeasible.
  • Missing modalities, client dropouts and heterogeneous edge hardware are explicitly left for future work.
  • Keeping raw labels local does not itself establish a formal privacy guarantee, and reported client GFLOPs do not establish end-to-end speed or energy gains.
